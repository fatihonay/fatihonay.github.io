Why the brain needs recurrence: RDM movies of a static image

5 November 2026 · Simulation · Methods

Look at a photo of a face. The photo does not change, but for about 300 ms after it appears, the way your visual cortex represents it changes again and again. First it reflects edges and textures, then "this is a face", then "this is something alive". How can a fixed input produce a representation that keeps moving? And what does that tell us about how the brain is wired?

Kietzmann and colleagues (2019, PNAS) answered this by combining MEG, the representational similarity analysis from the previous note, and deep neural networks. Their conclusion is in the title: recurrence is required.

RDM movies: RSA at every millisecond

Fifteen people viewed 92 images (human and animal faces and bodies, natural and man-made objects) while MEG recorded their brain activity. The signals were mapped onto three regions along the ventral visual stream: early visual cortex (V1–3), an intermediate stage (V4t/LO) and a high-level stage (IT/PHC). For each region and each time point, the authors computed an RDM. A sequence of RDMs over time is an RDM movie.

The movies were far from static. Low-level image structure peaked in V1–3 at about 100 ms. A face cluster appeared in all regions at about 130 ms. The animate–inanimate division appeared first in IT/PHC (about 140 ms), faded, and only later emerged in V4t/LO (about 260 ms): a reverse cascade, from high-level to intermediate. Analyses of information flow between regions found influence in both directions, feedforward and feedback.

How can a static input produce changing geometry?

In a feedforward network, each layer passes its result to the next one, once. Even if its units "ramp up" gradually, a layer's pattern only gets stronger; its shape, and so its RDM, stays the same. In a recurrent network, units also receive input from their neighbours in the same layer (lateral connections) and from higher layers (top-down connections). The activity keeps being reshaped, even though the image is fixed, so the representational geometry can evolve.

Training networks to reproduce the brain's movie

The demo uses networks with hand-set weights. The paper went further: it trained networks so that their RDM movies match the MEG ones, a method called dynamic representational distance learning (dRDL). Each network layer was assigned to one brain region, and the training objective rewarded matching that region's RDM at every time step (10 ms per step), plus a small object-recognition objective. To prevent the networks from simply memorising the 92 test images, they were trained on 141,000 other images and then tested on the original stimuli.

The comparison was carefully controlled. Recurrent networks (with lateral and top-down connections) were compared with ramping feedforward networks, which were allowed self-connections so their units could build up activity over time, and which had a similar number of parameters. The recurrent networks won clearly. For example, they followed the average pattern distance over time almost perfectly (correlations with held-out data of 0.95, 0.93 and 0.97 for V1–3, V4t/LO and IT/PHC, against 0.83, 0.59 and 0.47 for feedforward). They also matched the RDMs frame by frame better, predicted fMRI data from the same people better, and even recognised objects more accurately (about 74% against 64% correct).

Finally, the authors performed "virtual cooling": switching off lateral or top-down connections in trained networks, in the way cooling a brain area temporarily silences it. Both kinds of connection mattered, for recognition and for matching the brain.

What this does, and does not, show

Matching a brain's RDM movie is strong evidence that a model captures something real, but it is not proof that the brain computes the same way. Different mechanisms can produce similar RDMs, which is why the comparison against a well-matched control (ramping feedforward networks with similar parameter counts) is essential. Other good checks are testing on images the model never saw, testing whether results depend on how model time steps are aligned with milliseconds, and asking whether the model also predicts other data, as the paper did with fMRI.

Try it in Python

A tiny recurrent layer with a fixed input already produces an RDM movie (PyTorch):

import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
n_images, n_inputs, n_units, n_steps = 12, 16, 40, 20
x = torch.randn(n_images, n_inputs)                 # a fixed "image" for each stimulus
W_in = torch.randn(n_units, n_inputs) / n_inputs**0.5
W_rec = torch.randn(n_units, n_units) * 0.15        # lateral (recurrent) weights

def rdm(h):
    """1 - correlation between the patterns of every pair of stimuli."""
    return 1 - torch.corrcoef(h)

h = torch.zeros(n_images, n_units)
movie = []
for t in range(n_steps):                            # the input never changes...
    h = h + 0.3 * (-h + torch.relu(x @ W_in.T + h @ W_rec.T))
    movie.append(rdm(h))                            # ...but the RDM does

upper = torch.triu_indices(n_images, n_images, 1)
change = [torch.corrcoef(torch.stack([movie[t][upper[0], upper[1]],
                                      movie[-1][upper[0], upper[1]]]))[0, 1].item()
          for t in range(1, n_steps, 4)]
print([round(c, 2) for c in change])                # similarity of each frame to the last one

# The idea behind dRDL: a loss that rewards matching a target RDM movie
def rdm_loss(model_movie, brain_movie):
    loss = 0
    for M, B in zip(model_movie, brain_movie):
        m, b = M[upper[0], upper[1]], B[upper[0], upper[1]]
        loss = loss + 1 - torch.corrcoef(torch.stack([m, b]))[0, 1]
    return loss / len(model_movie)                  # minimise with backpropagation through time

Why this matters for EEG

This study is MEG, but the same analysis works with EEG: compute an RDM from the pattern across electrodes (or across reconstructed sources) at every time point, and you get an RDM movie of your own experiment. Its strength is time: EEG and MEG show when each representational distinction appears, and that timing is what reveals recurrent processing that a single snapshot, or a slow method like fMRI, would miss. Combined with models trained on brain data, it turns EEG from a description of brain activity into a test of how the brain computes.

Note: in the demo, the "brain" RDM movies are simulated from three components (low-level features, faces, animacy) whose strengths rise and fall over time following the qualitative pattern described in the paper; they are not the paper's data. The networks have three layers of 40 units with fixed, hand-set weights rather than trained ones; the recurrent circuitry is written in a simplified form (slowly self-amplifying category signals in the top layer, and top-down feedback into the middle layer).
