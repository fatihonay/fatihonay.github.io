How do neurons fall into rhythm?

15 October 2026 · Simulation · Oscillations

When you close your eyes and relax, an EEG electrode at the back of your head picks up a strong wave of about 10 cycles per second — the alpha rhythm. But no single neuron "plays" that rhythm. An electrode hears millions of cells at once, and their signals only add up to a clear wave if many of them oscillate together.

So how does a crowd of slightly different oscillators decide to march in step? A beautifully simple model by Yoshiki Kuramoto (1975) gives an answer: each oscillator gets a gentle pull towards the group's average phase. Below a critical pull strength, nothing happens. Above it, a rhythm suddenly appears out of noise.

Each dot is an oscillator moving around the circle. Colour = its natural speed. The arrow is the group average.

Glowing line: the summed "EEG" signal. Thin line: synchrony r (0 = chaos, 1 = perfect unison).

Try this: slowly drag K from 0 to 6. Around K ≈ 2.5 the dots start to clump and the summed signal grows from a fuzzy line into a clear wave. Then raise the noise and watch the rhythm break up again.

The model in one line

Each oscillator i has a phase θi and its own natural frequency ωi. The group's average is summarised by r (how aligned everyone is) and ψ (where the crowd is pointing): 

dθᵢ/dt = ωᵢ + K · r · sin(ψ − θᵢ) + noise

The second term pulls each oscillator towards the crowd — and the better the crowd is aligned (larger r), the stronger the pull. That feedback loop is why synchrony switches on so abruptly.

Run it yourself in Python

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

N, K, dt, steps = 200, 3.0, 0.01, 4000
omega = np.random.normal(2*np.pi, 1.5, N)     # natural frequencies
theta = np.random.uniform(0, 2*np.pi, N)      # random start

r_hist, eeg = [], []
for _ in range(steps):
    z = np.exp(1j*theta).mean()               # complex order parameter
    r, psi = np.abs(z), np.angle(z)
    theta += dt*(omega + K*r*np.sin(psi - theta))
    r_hist.append(r)
    eeg.append(np.cos(theta).mean())          # what an electrode would "hear"

plt.plot(eeg, lw=0.8, label="summed signal")
plt.plot(r_hist, lw=2, label="synchrony r")
plt.legend(); plt.show()

Why this matters

Changes in neural synchrony show up in many conditions — for example, altered rhythms and connectivity in Parkinson's disease, which is a large part of my own research. Simple models like Kuramoto's help us reason about what an EEG change might mean: are cells firing differently, or are they just less in step with each other?

Note: the simulation runs about 10× slower than real alpha so your eyes can follow it.
