From edges to objects: how the brain sees

26 October 2026 · Simulation · Vision

You recognise a friend's face in about a tenth of a second. Yet the first cells in your visual cortex don't see faces at all. They see tiny edges. In 1959, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel found that single neurons in primary visual cortex (V1) fire for a bar of light at one particular angle and stay quiet for the others. That discovery later earned them a Nobel Prize.

From there, information climbs a hierarchy: V1 → V2 → V4 → inferior temporal cortex (IT). At each step, neurons combine the outputs of the step below, so they respond to larger parts of the image and to more complex patterns: edges, then corners and curves, then object parts, and finally whole objects.

What each panel does

Edge cells. Four small filters slide over the image, one per angle. Each filter is a Gabor pattern, a close mathematical match to the receptive fields of V1 simple cells. A cell fires only when the image under it matches its preferred angle; weak or negative matches are cut to zero, just as a neuron can't fire "below zero".

Pooled edges. Each unit takes the strongest response in a small neighbourhood. The map gets coarser, but the edge is still detected if it shifts a little. V1 complex cells behave this way: they care about the angle more than the exact position.

Corner cells. Each unit fires when two different angles meet in the same place. This is a new feature built only by combining simpler ones. Neurons in V2 and V4 respond to corners, junctions and curves in the same way. You may notice that the two ends of a straight line also light up: the edge "fades out" there, which looks like a change of angle. Real visual cortex has end-stopped cells that respond to line endings in a similar way.

The same idea in a CNN

A convolutional neural network (CNN) is built from the same three steps, stacked many times: convolution (a bank of filters sliding over the image), a threshold (ReLU), and pooling. The difference is that a CNN learns its filters from millions of photos instead of having them designed by hand. Remarkably, trained networks end up with Gabor-like edge detectors in their first layer, much like V1.

You can see this yourself with a network trained on everyday photos:

import torchvision
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

net = torchvision.models.alexnet(weights="DEFAULT")   # trained on ImageNet
w = net.features[0].weight.detach()                    # 64 first-layer filters
w = (w - w.min()) / (w.max() - w.min())                # rescale for display

fig, axes = plt.subplots(8, 8, figsize=(6, 6))
for ax, f in zip(axes.flat, w):
    ax.imshow(f.permute(1, 2, 0))
    ax.axis("off")
plt.suptitle("Layer 1 of a trained CNN: edge and colour detectors")
plt.show()

The similarity goes deeper than layer 1. Deeper layers of trained CNNs predict the responses of neurons in V4 and IT (Yamins et al., 2014). In human MEG, early CNN layers match early brain responses and deep layers match later ones (Cichy et al., 2016).

Why this matters for EEG

When we study how EEG follows a natural video, we first have to describe the video with numbers. Simple descriptions, such as how much the pixels move, explain only a small part of the brain's response (Yao et al., 2024). CNN layers give a richer description, from edges up to objects, which we can line up against EEG recorded during viewing. That's the subject of an upcoming note.

Note: the demo uses four hand-made filters and simple pooling to show the principle. Real visual cortex and real CNNs have thousands of feature types and many more stages.
